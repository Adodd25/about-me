## The Unexpected Path

Most cybersecurity professionals start with computer science degrees and internships at tech companies. I started with a Kinesiology degree and a plan to become a physical therapist.

At **Morehouse College**, I studied the human body—anatomy, biomechanics, how systems work together. I also played baseball, which taught me discipline, preparation, and how to perform under pressure.

I thought I knew exactly where I was going. Then life happened.

---

## The Pivot

After graduation, I discovered cybersecurity almost by accident. A mentor suggested I look into it. I was skeptical—I didn't have a traditional tech background. But the more I learned, the more I realized something:

**Kinesiology and cybersecurity aren't that different.**

Both are about understanding complex systems. Both require diagnosing problems before they become critical. Both demand that you see how individual components connect to the whole.

Physical therapists protect the body. Security architects protect organizations. The mindset is the same.

I enrolled in the **University of Tampa's MS in Cybersecurity** program. I approached it the way I approached everything: outwork everyone. I graduated with a 3.93 GPA, Magna Cum Laude, and was named **Outstanding MS Student of the Year**.

The non-traditional background I thought was a weakness became my strength. I thought differently. I asked questions others didn't think to ask. I understood that security was about people, not just technology.

---

## Building a Career

**Protiviti** gave me my first professional security role. I conducted assessments for Fortune 500 companies and presented findings to C-suite executives. I learned how to translate technical risk into business language.

**Techstars** opened my eyes to venture capital. As a VC Fellow, I advised 20+ startups on security strategy and helped structure investor relations for a $150M fund. I saw how security could enable—or destroy—young companies.

**The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta** is where I found my calling. I oversee multifactor authentication across the Sixth District and design cloud security solutions for critical financial infrastructure. The work matters. When you're protecting the systems that underpin the American economy, there's no room for error.

---

## What's Next

I'm pursuing my **MBA at The Wharton School** because I believe the future of security leadership requires business acumen. The best CISOs aren't just technical experts—they're strategists who can align security with business objectives.

I'm also a **Senior Fellow at HBCUvc**, where I mentor founders and work to build inclusive pathways into venture capital. My unconventional path taught me that talent is everywhere, but opportunity isn't. I want to change that.

---

## What My Background Taught Me About Security

1. **Systems thinking transfers.** Understanding how the body works prepared me to understand how organizations work. Both have vulnerabilities. Both need protection.

2. **Diagnosis before treatment.** A good PT doesn't just treat symptoms. A good security architect doesn't just deploy tools. Both find root causes.

3. **Prevention beats intervention.** In physical therapy, you'd rather prevent injury than rehabilitate it. In security, you'd rather prevent breaches than respond to them.

4. **Communication is everything.** PTs explain complex medical concepts to patients. Security leaders explain complex risks to executives. Same skill, different domain.

5. **The human element matters.** Bodies are unpredictable. So are people. The best security accounts for human behavior, not just technical controls.
