## The Unexpected Path

Most cybersecurity professionals start with computer science degrees and internships at tech companies. I started with a baseball scholarship and a dream of playing professionally.

At **Morehouse College**, I studied Kinesiology while playing Division II baseball. I learned discipline, preparation, and how to perform under pressure. I learned that talent gets you in the door, but work ethic keeps you there.

Baseball didn't work out the way I planned. But it taught me something that would become central to my career in security: **anticipate what's coming, prepare relentlessly, and adapt when the pitch isn't what you expected.**

---

## The Pivot

After graduation, I discovered cybersecurity almost by accident. A mentor suggested I look into it. I was skeptical—I didn't have a traditional tech background. But the more I learned, the more I realized that security wasn't just about code. It was about understanding systems, anticipating threats, and protecting what matters.

I enrolled in the **University of Tampa's MS in Cybersecurity** program. I approached it the same way I approached baseball: outwork everyone. I graduated with a 3.93 GPA, Magna Cum Laude, and was named **Outstanding MS Student of the Year**.

The non-traditional background I thought was a weakness became my strength. I thought differently. I asked questions others didn't think to ask. I understood that security was about people, not just technology.

---

## Building a Career

**Protiviti** gave me my first professional security role. I conducted assessments for Fortune 500 companies and presented findings to C-suite executives. I learned how to translate technical risk into business language.

**Techstars** opened my eyes to venture capital. As a VC Fellow, I advised 20+ startups on security strategy and helped structure investor relations for a $150M fund. I saw how security could enable—or destroy—young companies.

**The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta** is where I found my calling. I oversee multifactor authentication across the Sixth District and design cloud security solutions for critical financial infrastructure. The work matters. When you're protecting the systems that underpin the American economy, there's no room for error.

---

## What's Next

I'm pursuing my **MBA at The Wharton School** because I believe the future of security leadership requires business acumen. The best CISOs aren't just technical experts—they're strategists who can align security with business objectives.

I'm also a **Senior Fellow at HBCUvc**, where I mentor founders and work to build inclusive pathways into venture capital. My unconventional path taught me that talent is everywhere, but opportunity isn't. I want to change that.

---

## What Baseball Taught Me About Security

1. **Preparation is everything.** A pitcher studies hitters. A security architect studies threat actors. Both are trying to anticipate what's coming.

2. **You will fail.** The best hitters fail 70% of the time. In security, breaches happen. What matters is how you respond and what you learn.

3. **Trust your team.** Baseball is nine people working together. Security is everyone in the organization working together. You can't do it alone.

4. **Stay calm under pressure.** Bases loaded, full count, two outs. A critical incident at 2 AM. Both require the same thing: composure.

5. **The game is always evolving.** New pitches, new strategies, new analytics. New threats, new technologies, new attack vectors. Never stop learning.
